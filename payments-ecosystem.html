How a payment moves

The key steps from initiation to settlement.

1 Initiate Payment

Customer pays (card/UPI/wallet) at the merchant.

2 Process & Secure

Gateway captures, secures and routes the request.

3 Authorise

Banks and network validate and approve the transaction.

4 Complete & Settle

Payment is confirmed and merchant funds are settled (T+0 to T+2, typically).

ⓘ  What is a Payment Gateway?

A technology platform that securely captures, authenticates and routes payment transactions between the merchant, banks and card networks, and helps settle the funds to the merchant. The gateway is one part of the payment stack; acquiring, processing and settlement may be handled by separate providers.

Infrastructure, Routing & Security

Payment Orchestration & Tokenisation

How modern enterprise payments achieve 99.99% processing resilience, dynamic cost routing and end-to-end credential security.

🔀

Multi-Acquirer Orchestration

Single-gateway dependency exposes merchants to downtime and processing bottlenecks. Payment orchestration layers sit above multiple payment aggregators (PAs) and banks, dynamically balancing traffic and switching between backup rails in real time.

⚡

Dynamic Smart Routing

Algorithms route transactions based on card BIN, issuer health metrics, latency and commercial processing costs (MDR). If an acquiring bank switch experiences elevated failure rates, the engine instantly re-routes authorization to a healthy alternate bank.

🛡️

Card-on-File Tokenisation (CoFT)

In accordance with RBI security guidelines, merchant platforms cannot store raw 16-digit card numbers (PAN) or CVVs. Token Requestors generate device and merchant-specific encrypted cryptograms via card networks (RuPay, Visa, Mastercard) for friction-free repeat checkout.

Key Pillars of Payment Switch Architecture
✔
Cascade Failover: Automatically retries dropped card or UPI transactions on secondary processors without forcing the customer to restart checkout.
✔
Zero Data at Rest Exposure: End-to-end PCI-DSS Level 1 compliance through hardware security modules (HSM) and symmetric AES-256 field-level encryption.
✔
Automated Multi-Rail Reconciliation: Three-way reconciliation matching internal order IDs against gateway settlement files and bank MIS feeds to eliminate revenue leakage.

UPI Architecture

UPI / TPAP: The Unified Payments Layer

What is TPAP? A Third Party Application Provider (TPAP) is an RBI/NPCI-recognised entity that provides a customer-facing payment application (e.g., PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm, WhatsApp Pay) enabling UPI transactions. Because TPAPs are not banks, they must partner with an authorised Sponsor PSP Bank to access NPCI’s core UPI switching and settlement network.

Ecosystem Landscape

Types of Banks in India

A diverse institutional banking fabric powering retail finance, enterprise commerce, and India’s digital payment rails.

🏛️ Public Sector Banks (PSBs)

Government-owned commercial banks with vast nationwide branch distribution and systemic infrastructure.

e.g., State Bank of India (SBI), PNB, BoB, Canara
🤝 Private Sector Banks

Privately owned, technologically advanced commercial banks leading digital innovation, merchant acquiring & corporate alliances.

e.g., HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank, Kotak
🌐 Foreign Banks

Overseas financial institutions operating branches in India, specialising in multinational enterprise banking, trade finance & cross-border flows.

e.g., HSBC, Standard Chartered, Citibank, Barclays
👥 Cooperative Banks

Member-owned credit institutions providing localized financial services to urban communities, agriculture, and rural markets.

e.g., Saraswat Bank, Cosmos Bank, Shamrao Vithal
🌾 Small Finance Banks (SFBs)

RBI-chartered institutions with a dedicated mandate for financial inclusion, unserved micro-enterprises & MSME lending.

e.g., AU Small Finance, Ujjivan SFB, Equitas SFB
📱 Payments Banks (PBs)

Differentiated banks authorized for payments, domestic remittances, savings deposits up to ₹2 Lakh, and PPI wallets; no direct lending.

e.g., Airtel Payments Bank, India Post PB, Fino

Who pays for the rate?

Rate subvention is a commercial arrangement where the cost of acceptance (MDR) is partly or fully funded by a third party such as a brand, bank or platform.

MerchantPays a lower or zero MDR
+
Brand / PlatformSubsidises part of the cost (e.g. 0.5–1.0%)
+
BankMay co-fund as part of a scheme

The payer split is contract-specific. Funding can be fixed, percentage-based, capped, campaign-linked or conditional on transaction performance.

KNOW THE ECOSYSTEM

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a Payment Gateway and a Payment Aggregator?

A Payment Gateway (PG) is the technological layer that securely captures, encrypts, and routes transaction data. A Payment Aggregator (PA) is an RBI-authorized entity that boards merchants, pools transaction funds in an escrow account, and completes merchant settlements.

How does MDR (Merchant Discount Rate) work?

MDR is the fee charged to a merchant for accepting digital payments. It is typically split among the card issuer bank (interchange fee), card network (switch fee), acquiring bank, and payment aggregator or gateway.

What is Rate Subvention in merchant pricing?

Rate Subvention is a commercial arrangement where the cost of payment acceptance (MDR or EMI interest) is partly or fully sponsored by a third-party brand, platform, or financial partner rather than the merchant alone.

What is a Third Party Application Provider (TPAP) in UPI?

A TPAP is a non-bank application (such as Google Pay, PhonePe, Paytm or WhatsApp Pay) that facilitates customer UPI payments by partnering with an authorised Sponsor PSP Bank. The sponsor bank provides connectivity and settlement access to NPCI’s central UPI switch.

What is the core difference between a PPI wallet and a Bank account?

A Prepaid Payment Instrument (PPI) allows customers to store preloaded funds in a digital wallet or prepaid card for transactions, with funds held in escrow with a scheduled bank. Unlike full commercial banks, PPI issuers cannot pay interest on balances, open savings/current accounts, or extend direct credit or loans from customer balances.

What are Merchant Category Codes (MCCs) and why are they important?

An MCC is a standardised 4-digit code defined by ISO 18245 and card schemes (Visa, Mastercard, RuPay) to classify a merchant’s primary line of business. MCCs determine applicable interchange fees, MDR pricing tiers, chargeback rules, risk monitoring thresholds, and reward point eligibility on credit cards.
